Professional Chimney Inspection in San Francisco, CA

A professional chimney inspection is essential before using your fireplace, when buying or selling a San Francisco home, and after any chimney event such as a fire, earthquake, or severe storm.

Types of Chimney Inspections We Perform

AL Chimney Service San Francisco performs all three levels of chimney inspection in accordance with NFPA 211 standards — the same standards followed by certified chimney professionals across the United States.

Level 1 Chimney Inspection

A Level 1 inspection covers all accessible portions of the chimney exterior and interior, including the firebox, smoke chamber, damper, and visible flue sections. This is the standard annual inspection recommended for chimneys that have been in continuous service with the same appliance and no significant changes. It verifies that the chimney is structurally sound, free of obstructions, and free of combustible deposits.

Level 2 Chimney Inspection

A Level 2 inspection includes everything in a Level 1, plus a video scan of the entire flue interior using a specialized camera. This level is required when:

  • Buying or selling a home in San Francisco
  • Changing the fuel type or appliance connected to the chimney
  • After a chimney fire or natural disaster
  • After any event that may have caused structural damage

We provide full video footage and a written report documenting all findings — valuable documentation for real estate transactions and insurance claims.

Level 3 Chimney Inspection

A Level 3 inspection is performed when a serious structural defect is suspected. It may involve removal of components such as the chimney crown, interior walls, or other elements to access and fully evaluate the condition of the chimney. This level is reserved for situations where Level 1 or 2 inspection reveals conditions that cannot be fully assessed otherwise.

What We Check During an Inspection

  • Firebox — condition of firebrick and refractory panels
  • Damper — operation, seating, and corrosion
  • Smoke shelf and smoke chamber
  • Flue lining — cracks, spalling, mortar joint deterioration
  • Chimney crown — cracks and water infiltration
  • Chimney cap — presence, fit, and condition
  • Exterior masonry — spalling, efflorescence, mortar erosion
  • Flashing — seal at roof-chimney junction
  • Creosote buildup level and classification

Why Every San Francisco Homeowner Needs a Chimney Inspection

Here's a conversation we have regularly: a homeowner lights their fireplace for the first time in a few years, everything seems fine, and they wonder why anyone would bother paying for an inspection. The fireplace worked, didn't it? The answer is that most chimney hazards are completely invisible to the naked eye — and some of the most dangerous ones show zero warning signs right up until something goes seriously wrong.

A cracked flue liner, for example, is not something you'll see from the firebox opening. Those cracks — which can allow carbon monoxide and combustion gases to seep into your walls and living areas — are only visible through a video camera inserted into the flue. A deteriorated mortar joint inside the smoke chamber won't affect how the fire looks or burns until water infiltration has been weakening the surrounding masonry for years. An improperly sized or corroded damper can allow dangerous back-drafting of gases while the fire appears to be burning normally.

The simple truth is that the only way to know whether your chimney is actually safe is to have it professionally inspected. This is not a sales pitch — it's the consistent position of the National Fire Protection Association (NFPA 211), the Chimney Safety Institute of America, and virtually every fire safety authority in the United States. Annual inspection is the standard of care for good reason.

In San Francisco specifically, where homes are older, seismic activity is a constant reality, and marine humidity drives corrosion year-round, the inspection imperative is even stronger. We find significant, previously undetected defects in a meaningful percentage of chimneys we inspect — including in homes where the fireplace has been "working fine" for years.

What Happens During a Level 2 Video Scan

The Level 2 inspection with video scan is our most requested service and the one we most commonly recommend for San Francisco homeowners who haven't had a recent inspection. Here's exactly what the process looks like so you know what to expect.

  1. Firebox and lower flue assessment: We begin with a thorough visual and tactile examination of the firebox — checking the condition of the firebrick, refractory panels, smoke shelf, and damper. We're looking for cracks, erosion, heat damage, and corrosion.
  2. Camera insertion: A high-resolution camera on a flexible rod is inserted into the flue from the firebox opening. The camera transmits live footage to a monitor, which we review with you in real time. Every inch of the flue interior is captured.
  3. Flue liner examination: We look for cracks, missing mortar joints, spalled tile sections, offset joints, and any location where the liner continuity is broken. Even small cracks are significant — combustion gases exploit every gap.
  4. Smoke chamber inspection: The smoke chamber above the damper is assessed for parging quality, crack formation, and creosote accumulation on rough surfaces.
  5. Exterior and attic-accessible areas: We examine the chimney crown, cap, flashing, and all exterior masonry accessible from the roof or attic. Flashing is a common source of water intrusion in San Francisco homes.
  6. Video documentation: The complete video footage is saved and provided to you along with the written report. This documentation is immediately useful for insurance claims, real estate disclosures, and repair planning.
  7. Written report: You receive a written summary of all findings, classified by severity, with our recommended course of action. We explain everything in plain language — no jargon, no pressure.

The entire appointment typically takes 60 to 90 minutes. You're welcome to observe the entire inspection, and we encourage questions. The more you understand about your chimney's condition, the better positioned you are to make informed decisions about maintenance and repair.

Chimney Inspection When Buying or Selling a San Francisco Home

If you're buying a home in San Francisco, the chimney inspection is one of the most important due diligence steps you can take — and it's one that general home inspectors routinely miss or underperform. General inspectors are trained to assess dozens of systems and are not chimney specialists. Their standard practice is a visual check of the firebox and exterior, which cannot reveal liner cracks, smoke chamber deterioration, or internal moisture damage.

A NFPA 211 Level 2 inspection performed by a CSIA-certified chimney professional gives you the complete picture before you close. If defects are found, you have documented evidence to negotiate repairs or a price reduction with the seller. If the chimney is in good condition, you have peace of mind and documentation you can use going forward.

If you're selling, a pre-listing chimney inspection is equally valuable. Buyers' agents in San Francisco increasingly request chimney documentation, and having a clean inspection report in hand can prevent last-minute deal complications. If repairs are needed, you can address them on your own timeline before listing rather than scrambling to satisfy a buyer's contingency under contract pressure.

  • Pre-purchase inspections protect buyers from expensive post-close surprises
  • Pre-listing inspections help sellers price and disclose accurately
  • Video documentation provides legal protection for both parties
  • Level 2 inspection is the NFPA-required standard for real estate transactions
  • Our written reports are formatted for inclusion in real estate disclosure packages

How Often Should You Get a Chimney Inspection?

The NFPA 211 standard is clear: chimneys should be inspected at least annually, regardless of whether they're used frequently or occasionally. This is not an arbitrary recommendation — it reflects the fact that chimneys can deteriorate, develop blockages, and sustain damage between uses, even during seasons when they're not being fired.

For San Francisco homeowners, our specific guidance is:

  • All fireplace types: Annual inspection, ideally before the start of the burn season in fall
  • After any earthquake of 3.5 magnitude or greater: Level 2 inspection to check for seismic liner damage before next use
  • After a chimney fire: Level 2 or Level 3 inspection before the chimney is used again
  • When buying or selling a home: Level 2 inspection as part of due diligence
  • When changing appliances or fuel types: Level 2 inspection to confirm the flue is appropriately sized and lined
  • After any significant storm or wind event: Visual inspection of exterior components, cap, and crown

If your chimney hasn't been inspected in more than two years, we'd recommend scheduling sooner rather than later. The longer a defect goes undetected, the more damage it typically causes — and the more expensive the eventual repair.

What We Find Most Often in San Francisco Chimneys

After years of inspecting chimneys across San Francisco and the Bay Area, certain patterns emerge. These are the issues we encounter most frequently:

  • Cracked clay tile liners: Extremely common in homes built before 1970. Clay tile is brittle, prone to thermal shock, and vulnerable to seismic movement. Many cracks are hairline but still represent significant gas migration risks.
  • Deteriorated mortar joints: San Francisco's persistent moisture accelerates mortar erosion. Joints in the smoke chamber, firebox, and exterior masonry are frequent problem areas.
  • Missing or damaged chimney caps: Without a cap, San Francisco fog, rain, and marine air pour directly into the flue. We find uncapped chimneys regularly — sometimes the cap blew off in wind and the homeowner didn't know.
  • Failed flashing: The joint between the chimney and roof is a chronic leak source. Failed flashing leads to water damage in attics, walls, and ceilings — often blamed on roof problems when the chimney is actually the culprit.
  • Glazed creosote: Third-degree creosote requiring chemical treatment is disproportionately common in San Francisco homes where fires are burned during short, concentrated seasonal windows with unseasoned wood.
  • Seismic displacement: Even minor earthquakes can shift flue tiles out of alignment. We use video inspection to identify displaced sections that create gaps in the liner.
  • Animal nests: Raccoons, birds, and squirrels regularly enter uncapped flues. Nests create blockages and introduce organic debris that contributes to moisture problems.
