Why Dryer Vent Cleaning Is Critical

The U.S. Fire Administration reports that dryers cause approximately 2,900 residential fires per year, with the leading cause being failure to clean. Lint, a highly flammable material, accumulates in the dryer duct system over time. As it builds up, it restricts airflow, causing the dryer to overheat and potentially ignite.

Signs Your Dryer Vent Needs Cleaning

  • Clothes take more than one cycle to dry completely
  • The dryer feels unusually hot to the touch during operation
  • You can smell burning during dryer operation
  • The vent flap outside the home doesn't open when the dryer runs
  • It has been more than 12 months since the last cleaning
  • You dry heavy items like towels and bedding frequently

Our Dryer Vent Cleaning Process

  1. Disconnect dryer and access duct at the wall connection
  2. Use rotary brush system to clear the entire duct length
  3. High-powered vacuum to remove all lint from the duct
  4. Inspect the exterior vent termination for blockages and proper operation
  5. Check for any duct damage, improper materials (foil flex), or code issues
  6. Reconnect dryer and test operation

The Hidden Fire Risk Most San Francisco Homeowners Don't Know About

Here's a statistic that surprises most people: clothes dryers cause roughly 2,900 house fires in the United States every year, and the number one cause isn't a mechanical failure — it's simply failure to clean the vent. Lint is one of the most flammable household materials there is. Every time you run a load of laundry, your dryer pushes a stream of hot air (and the lint that comes off your clothes) through a duct that runs from the dryer to the outside of your building. Over time, lint sticks to the inside of that duct. When enough builds up, it restricts airflow, causes the dryer to overheat, and eventually ignites.

This risk is particularly acute in San Francisco for a few reasons. First, many SF homes — especially Victorians, Edwardians, and older apartment buildings — have long, convoluted dryer vent runs that go through floors, ceilings, and walls before reaching an exterior termination. Longer duct runs mean more surface area for lint to accumulate. Second, San Francisco's high-density housing stock means many buildings have shared or stacked laundry systems where vents run through multiple units before exiting the building. In those configurations, a clog doesn't just affect one unit — it creates a fire risk for the entire building. Third, a lot of older construction in SF still uses flexible foil duct — the accordion-style silver duct material — which is now prohibited by code because its ridged interior catches lint much more readily than smooth rigid metal duct.

What Happens If You Ignore a Clogged Dryer Vent

The consequences of an uncleaned dryer vent go beyond fire risk alone, though that's certainly the most serious concern. Here's the full picture of what happens when the vent is clogged or partially blocked:

  • Longer dry times: Your dryer needs adequate airflow to work. When lint blocks the duct, hot moist air can't escape efficiently, and your clothes take two or three cycles to dry fully. That's wasted electricity or gas every single load.
  • Overheating: Modern dryers have thermal fuses and high-limit thermostats that shut the dryer down when it overheats. A frequently overheating dryer — caused by a clogged vent — will burn through these components. A thermal fuse replacement costs $100–$200 in parts and labor. A dryer vent cleaning costs less and prevents the problem entirely.
  • Shortened appliance life: Running the dryer under restricted-airflow conditions puts constant strain on the motor, heating element, and drum bearings. It's the equivalent of driving your car with the parking brake partially engaged. Dryers that run in well-maintained vent systems last years longer than those that don't.
  • Mold and moisture problems: If the vent termination is blocked — by a bird nest, a stuck flap, or debris buildup — moist air from the dryer can backdraft into the duct and into the wall or ceiling cavity, creating conditions for mold growth. In San Francisco's already-damp climate, this is a real and expensive problem.
  • Carbon monoxide risk: For gas dryers, a blocked vent can cause combustion gases to backdraft into the laundry area. Carbon monoxide poisoning from a blocked gas dryer vent is rare but has been documented. It's an invisible, odorless risk that a vent cleaning eliminates.

How Long Does Dryer Vent Cleaning Take?

This is one of the most common questions we get, and the honest answer is: usually about 45 minutes to 90 minutes for a standard residential vent. The time depends primarily on the length and complexity of the duct run.

For a typical San Francisco home or condo with a dryer vent that runs straight through an exterior wall, the cleaning is fast. For a Victorian flat with a dryer in a back bedroom and a vent that goes through multiple floors to the side of the building, it takes longer. We use a professional rotary brush system paired with a high-powered vacuum — this combination cleans the duct from inside the wall without requiring any drywall removal. After the cleaning, we inspect the exterior termination, confirm the vent flap is opening properly, check for any damaged sections of duct, and test the dryer's operation before we leave.

For most homes in San Francisco, we recommend annual dryer vent cleaning. If you dry heavy items frequently — towels, bedding, pet blankets — or if you have a long duct run, more frequent cleaning may be appropriate. We'll let you know after the inspection what we think makes sense for your specific setup.

Apartment and Condo Dryer Vent Cleaning in SF

San Francisco's dense housing stock creates unique dryer vent challenges for multi-unit buildings. In many apartment and condo buildings, individual unit dryers share a common exhaust chase — a vertical duct shaft that collects exhaust from multiple floors before exiting the building at the roofline. These shared systems accumulate lint at a much faster rate than single-family home vents and pose a proportionally larger fire risk, since a fire in the duct shaft can spread to multiple units rapidly.

Property managers and HOA boards have a genuine liability exposure when shared dryer vent systems aren't maintained. If a fire originates from a neglected common-area duct, and there's no documented maintenance history, the liability picture gets complicated quickly. We offer maintenance programs specifically designed for multi-unit buildings in San Francisco, including annual inspections and cleanings, documentation for your records, and notification to individual unit owners about any vent configurations we find that don't meet current code.

We also handle the specific challenge of older SF buildings that have converted from common laundry rooms to in-unit laundry — a conversion that often involves running new vent paths through the building's existing structure. If your building went through that kind of retrofit, it's worth having the vent paths inspected. Not all of them were done correctly.

Choosing the Right Dryer Vent Materials

Not all dryer vent duct materials are created equal, and if we find a problem during your cleaning, we'll tell you about it clearly. Here's what you need to know:

  • Rigid metal duct (best): Smooth-wall galvanized steel or aluminum duct is the gold standard for dryer venting. It's the easiest to clean, accumulates lint the most slowly, and is fully code-compliant for any length of run.
  • Semi-rigid metal duct (acceptable): Semi-rigid aluminum flex duct with a relatively smooth interior is acceptable for short duct runs and transition sections connecting the dryer to the wall. It needs to be installed without sharp kinks or compression.
  • Foil flex duct (not acceptable — replace it): The old accordion-style foil duct found in many older SF homes is prohibited by current building codes because its ridged interior catches lint at a much higher rate. If we find foil flex in your system, we'll recommend replacing it — and we can handle that as part of the service call.
  • Plastic duct (never acceptable): White plastic duct is a fire waiting to happen. It melts and burns when lint ignites inside it. If your dryer is connected to plastic duct, stop using it until it's replaced.

How to Maintain Your Dryer Vent Between Cleanings

Professional cleaning once a year handles the bulk of the lint buildup in your duct. Between cleanings, there are a few things you can do to keep your dryer running efficiently and safely:

  1. Clean the lint trap after every single load — every time. This is the single most impactful thing you can do. A full lint trap pushes more lint into the duct.
  2. Periodically go outside and check that the vent flap opens when the dryer is running. If it doesn't open fully, the duct may be clogged or the flap may be stuck or broken.
  3. Don't use wire mesh covers on the exterior vent termination — these clog quickly and create a fire risk. A proper vent cap with a flap is the right solution.
  4. Be aware of how long your dryer is taking. If a normal load suddenly takes significantly longer to dry, that's your early warning that the vent needs attention before a problem develops.
  5. If you hear a change in your dryer's sound, or notice it feels hotter than usual on the outside, stop using it and call us. Overheating is a serious warning sign.
