Why Chimney Relining Is Critical

The chimney liner serves three essential functions: it contains combustion gases and sparks, protects the surrounding masonry from heat, and provides the correct size flue for efficient draft. When a liner is cracked, corroded, or missing, combustion gases including deadly carbon monoxide can enter your San Francisco home's living spaces.

California building codes and insurance requirements typically mandate that a functioning liner be in place for all fireplace and heating appliance installations.

Types of Chimney Liners We Install

Stainless Steel Flexible Liner

The most commonly installed liner type, flexible stainless steel liners can navigate offsets and bends in the flue. They are available in single-wall and insulated double-wall configurations for different fuel types and appliance requirements.

Stainless Steel Rigid Liner

Rigid liners offer the smoothest interior surface for maximum draft performance. Used in straight chimneys, they are ideal for high-efficiency wood stoves and gas appliances.

Cast-in-Place Liner

A pumped, cast-in-place liner creates a seamless, insulated flue inside the existing masonry. Ideal for chimneys with irregular flue shapes or severely deteriorated interiors.

When Is Relining Required?

  • Cracked, spalled, or missing clay tile liner sections
  • Switching from one fuel type to another (wood to gas, etc.)
  • Installing a new high-efficiency appliance that requires a different flue size
  • After a chimney fire that damaged the existing liner
  • Older San Francisco homes where the original liner has deteriorated

The Problem with Old Clay Tile Liners in San Francisco Homes

If your San Francisco home was built before 1970 — and a huge proportion of the city's housing stock was — there's a very good chance your chimney is lined with clay tile. Clay tile became the standard liner material in American chimneys during the early twentieth century and remained dominant for decades. The tiles themselves can last many years under ideal conditions, but ideal conditions are not what San Francisco's chimneys experience.

Clay tile is a brittle material with poor resistance to thermal shock. When a fireplace goes from cold to extremely hot during a fire and then back to cold again, clay tiles expand and contract. Over years and decades of this cycling, micro-cracks develop. Small cracks eventually become larger fractures. Mortar joints between tile sections erode from moisture and the sulfuric compounds present in creosote residue. And in earthquake country, even minor seismic events introduce lateral stresses that clay tile — designed to handle only vertical loads — handles poorly.

The result, in many older San Francisco homes, is a flue liner with compromised integrity: cracks, gaps, displaced tiles, and eroded joints that create pathways for combustion gases — including carbon monoxide — to migrate through the surrounding masonry and into living spaces. A liner doesn't have to be catastrophically destroyed to be dangerous. A hairline crack that's invisible from the firebox opening can still allow CO to seep through the masonry wall cavity over months of use.

San Francisco's persistent moisture makes this worse. Water infiltrates existing micro-cracks, and when temperatures drop overnight, that water expands as it approaches freezing, wedging cracks wider. Salt compounds from marine air attack the clay itself and the mortar joints that hold tiles in place. Over the decades, this process turns a sound original liner into one that can no longer be trusted to contain combustion gases safely.

The solution is relining. A new liner — properly sized, properly installed, and made of modern materials — restores your chimney to full safety and code compliance regardless of the condition of the original clay tile it replaces.

Understanding Your Liner Options

Chimney relining is not a one-size-fits-all proposition. The right liner for your San Francisco home depends on the fuel type, appliance, chimney geometry, and your specific goals. Here's a clear breakdown of each option:

Stainless Steel Flexible Liner: The Most Versatile Choice

Flexible stainless steel liners are the most commonly installed liner type in San Francisco and for good reason: they can navigate the offsets, bends, and irregular flue paths common in older masonry chimneys. San Francisco's Victorian and Edwardian homes often have chimneys that were not built to perfectly straight specifications, and a rigid liner cannot accommodate that geometry. A flexible liner can.

These liners are available in Type 304 stainless steel (suitable for gas and oil appliances) and Type 316L stainless steel (the preferred choice for wood-burning applications due to better resistance to the acidic compounds in wood smoke condensate). Both types are available in single-wall and insulated double-wall configurations — insulated liners are recommended for exterior chimneys or any installation where maintaining flue gas temperature is important for proper draft.

Flexible liners are sized to match the specific appliance requirements, which matters significantly. An oversized flue — common when a modern gas insert replaces an old open-hearth fireplace — produces poor draft, excessive condensation, and accelerated creosote buildup. A properly sized liner solves all of these problems simultaneously.

Stainless Steel Rigid Liner: Maximum Draft Performance

In straight chimneys, rigid stainless steel liners offer the smoothest interior surface of any liner option. The smooth wall minimizes turbulence in the rising gas column, producing the best possible draft. For high-efficiency wood stoves and pellet appliances where optimal draft is critical to performance, a rigid liner is often the preferred choice.

The limitation is geometry: rigid liners cannot accommodate any significant offset or bend in the flue path. For the subset of San Francisco chimneys with truly straight flues, this is an excellent option. A Level 2 inspection will confirm whether your flue qualifies.

Cast-in-Place Liner: The Structural Restoration Option

Cast-in-place liners are installed by pumping a wet, insulating refractory material into the existing flue around a removable form. As the material cures, it creates a seamless, monolithic liner bonded to the surrounding masonry. This approach has several advantages that make it particularly well-suited to certain San Francisco chimneys:

  • Creates a completely seamless flue with no joints or connections — the most structurally continuous liner option
  • Works in flues with irregular shapes that are difficult to line with prefabricated products
  • Provides structural reinforcement to a deteriorated masonry chimney in addition to relining
  • Excellent insulation properties that improve draft and reduce condensation
  • Long service life when properly installed — typically warranted for 20+ years

The cast-in-place approach typically costs more than steel liner installation and requires more preparation and curing time. For chimneys with significant structural deterioration or complex flue geometry, it can be the most sensible long-term investment.

What Happens If You Don't Reline a Damaged Liner

This is the question that matters most, and we want to answer it honestly rather than just alarmingly. The consequences of using a chimney with a compromised liner depend on the severity and location of the damage, but they are serious.

The most immediate risk is carbon monoxide intrusion. When the liner is breached, combustion gases — including CO — can migrate through the surrounding masonry and into wall cavities, attic spaces, or directly into living areas. CO is colorless, odorless, and impossible to detect without a functioning carbon monoxide detector. Even low chronic exposure causes symptoms often mistaken for the flu: headaches, fatigue, nausea, and confusion. Acute high exposure is fatal. This is not a theoretical risk — it's a documented mechanism of real injury and death in homes with compromised chimney liners.

The second risk is fire. Even a small gap in a liner exposes surrounding masonry to radiant heat and direct contact with flame. Masonry is not combustible, but the wooden framing, flooring, and wall materials surrounding the chimney absolutely are. A liner failure that allows flames to reach adjacent framing can start a concealed structural fire — one that may burn inside walls or in the attic before ever being detected.

The third consequence is accelerating deterioration. A compromised liner allows moisture, acidic gases, and thermal stress to act directly on the surrounding masonry rather than being contained within the liner system. This dramatically accelerates chimney deterioration, turning what might have been a manageable repair situation into a full chimney rebuilding project if left long enough.

San Francisco Building Codes and Insurance Requirements

California building codes require that all fireplace and venting appliance installations have a properly functioning liner in place. This applies to new installations and to appliance upgrades — if you're replacing an old open-hearth fireplace with a gas insert or a new wood stove, code compliance typically requires either verifying the existing liner is intact and properly sized or installing a new liner appropriate to the new appliance.

Homeowner's insurance in California increasingly requires documented chimney condition. Some insurers require proof of recent inspection and certification of liner integrity before issuing or renewing policies on homes with fireplaces. A failed relining situation — discovered after a fire claim — can potentially complicate the claims process significantly. Having documented evidence that your chimney was properly inspected and relined is genuine financial protection.

Our Relining Process: From Assessment to Completion

  1. Level 2 video inspection: Every relining project starts with a complete video inspection of the existing flue to document current conditions, measure the flue dimensions accurately, and identify any pre-existing issues that need to be addressed before or during relining.
  2. Liner selection: Based on the inspection findings, your appliance type, flue geometry, and your goals, we recommend the most appropriate liner option and explain the reasoning. You make the final decision with full information.
  3. Flue preparation: If existing clay tile sections are displaced, crumbling, or otherwise obstructing the installation, they are addressed before the new liner goes in. For cast-in-place systems, the flue is cleaned and prepared to accept the refractory material properly.
  4. Liner installation: The new liner is installed per manufacturer specifications and in accordance with applicable building codes. For steel liners, proper sizing, connection at the appliance collar, and cap installation are all part of the installation. For cast-in-place, the material is mixed, pumped, and cured under controlled conditions.
  5. Top and bottom termination: Every liner installation includes proper termination at both ends — a correctly sized liner cap at the top to prevent water and animal entry, and proper connection to the appliance or adapter plate at the firebox.
  6. Post-installation inspection: After installation, we perform a final inspection to verify liner continuity, confirm proper clearances, and check all connections and terminations.
  7. Documentation: You receive a written record of the installation including all specifications, product information, and our inspection findings before and after. This documentation is yours to keep.
